54 Palace rifles: where a family connection meets public responsibility

The UN report names a security official and a family connection. The public role and the weapons trail must be examined together.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-15

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

Weapons intended to protect the state can turn against the public when custody controls fail. The UN experts’ 2024 report provides a specific example: 54 police-owned Galil automatic rifles diverted from National Palace stocks between 2013 and 2016. [1]

Paragraph 80 identifies Pierre-Léon Junior Saint Remy as responsible for Palace security and management of its weapons stockpile during that period. The experts describe him as a close relative of President Michel Martelly’s wife. The relationship matters because it accompanies a public role with responsibility for weapons. [1]

The next page is essential: information supplied to the experts indicates the rifles went to individuals, including gang members, in exchange for support for the president. The report says some were subsequently recovered at crime scenes or from gang members in Haiti and abroad. [1]

The report also describes a wider weakness: police leadership had been unable to inventory Palace stocks for years. The issue therefore extends beyond personal proximity to who kept the records, authorized transfers and checked what remained. [1]

Kinship alone does not prove Martelly or his wife issued an order. Personal responsibility requires further evidence. The security role and specified weapons, however, provide a concrete starting point for accountability.

Sources and verification limits

  1. S/2024/704 — Palace weapons, paragraphs 77–82, pages 20–21

    UN Security Council Panel of Experts · 2024-09-30

    Reissued with corrections October 29, 2024. Family relationships and security responsibilities are as described by the report; they do not automatically establish another person’s orders or guilt.
