Opinion: a quiet neighbourhood is not proof of freedom

Election security should assess whether people can speak and resist coercion, including places with few reported incidents.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-16

An editorial argument. Its judgments are not official findings about candidates.

A neighbourhood where people do not speak is not necessarily one without fear. Coercion can keep residents quiet and reported incidents low. We believe freedom to act belongs in election-readiness assessment.

For Croix-des-Bouquets, BINUH’s July–September 2026 report warns that relatively limited victim figures do not establish improved security, linking the situation to gang control over residents. This is the report’s assessment, not a neighbourhood survey conducted by us. [1]

For elections, we would assess whether people can assemble without armed-group permission, choose a different candidate and complain without retaliation. These are proposed measures needing verification that does not endanger respondents.

Déus Deronneth seeks a results-based security timetable. Wilner Joseph emerged from a 2026 VIKTWA nomination. Neither position alone answers whether people disagreeing with them can participate with equal freedom. [2] [3]

A sound assessment would disclose its methods and limits while protecting vulnerable people. Less noise may be good news. Calling it freedom requires knowing whether residents remain able to say no.
