Savannah Savary vs Olivier Barrau: family businesses and the public interest

Their business histories explain part of their careers. The public question concerns what they control today and the safeguards they accept for tomorrow.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-15

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

Inheriting a business is no offence. Nor does it guarantee an ability to govern. For Savannah Savary and Olivier Barrau, family and business belong in the public record where they help explain the financial interests of someone seeking power.

In an October 2026 Haitian Times interview, Savary says she inherited Distillerie 1928 and Habitation Robert and that the businesses employ more than 100 people. That is her account, not an independently verified employment total. She declined financial details, citing fear of becoming a target. [1]

AIC’s 2022 annual report says Barrau took over management of the family insurance brokerage after his studies. It identifies Lucien Barrau as his father and dates AIC’s creation to December 2001. This documents business history and a family relationship; it supplies no complete inventory of his current assets. [2]

The sources have different limits. For Savary, there is her interview account. For Barrau, there is the company’s history. Neither is an independent personal financial audit. One candidate’s employment claim and the other company’s founding date cannot rank their wealth or effectiveness.

The common test starts with state decisions: who would manage the businesses during a term, and who would decide on a contract or benefit affecting them? A process preventing personal interests influencing public decisions is more useful than a list of relatives without documented roles. These sources establish no unlawful contract. They show why financial-interest safeguards should precede taking power.
