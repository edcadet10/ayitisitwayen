Etzer Emile: 41 files and a promise for his first 100 days

He promises resources for existing justice units. BINUH’s record provides a baseline for examining that pledge.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-16

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

An opened door is not the same as an advancing case. For people awaiting justice after a massacre, a new office can be a beginning. What follows matters: evidence reaching a court, protected witnesses and a decision free from political pressure.

BINUH reports two units for mass crimes and financial crimes, created in April 2025 and launched in May 2026: 24 magistrates assigned and 41 files identified and reviewed as potentially within their jurisdiction. Magistrates include judges and prosecutors; prosecutors bring cases for the State. [1]

The same report records no progress that quarter in proceedings on Grand Ravine (2017), La Saline (2018), Bel Air (2019) and Pont-Sondé (2024). That describes July–September 2026, not every possible later development. [1]

Both findings can be true: staffing and institutions advanced while major proceedings stalled. The 41 is not a count of judgments, convictions or resolved massacres. The passage does not list those files; we cannot assume the four massacre cases are already among them.

Etzer Emile’s programme makes a specific commitment: within his first 100 days these units would receive resources to operate fully. It recognizes that they already exist. That supplies a measurable pledge: how much funding, which equipment, what protection and which delivery dates? The programme is a plan, not proof of delivery. [2]

Measurement should distinguish approved resources, actual spending and delivery to the service. Follow-up can then show case stages without exposing witnesses or protected evidence. These are proposed measures; we do not say every case must conclude within 100 days.

The election should make this distinction clear: a president can answer for resources supplied to justice without choosing the verdict. For victims and accused people, the answer should come from a system examining evidence and deciding according to law.
