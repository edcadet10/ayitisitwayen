Tardieu’s library promise should be judged from a reader’s chair

Putting books on a computer can help. The public service begins when a young reader can actually use them.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-18

An editorial argument. Its judgments are not official findings about candidates.

On January 25, 2017, Jerry Tardieu’s account described a meeting with Josette Bruffaerts of Haïti Futur and proposed working together to digitise the Pétion-Ville library. Its wording described work to come, not a completed installation. [1]

The useful electoral test starts with an imagined reader, not a machine. Can a sixteen-year-old find the book needed for school? Can someone without a phone or money for data read it? Can a librarian help? A digital project earns public value through those ordinary encounters.

Digitisation also covers different tasks. A searchable list tells readers which books exist; a scanned book lets them read pages. Neither automatically supplies electricity, accessible equipment, trained staff or permission to distribute copyrighted texts. A promise should say which problem it intends to solve.

This review did not recover a signed project agreement, delivery record or usage report. Targeted searches also did not establish that specific project’s outcome. That is a limit of this review, not proof that no work occurred or a verdict on Haïti Futur’s wider activities.

I would ask Tardieu for a simple before-and-after account: what readers could do before, what changed, when it opened, what it cost and who kept it running. Publish the catalogue and service hours alongside it. The achievement worth claiming is easier access to knowledge, demonstrated where readers sit.
