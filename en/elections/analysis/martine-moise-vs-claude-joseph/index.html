Martine Moïse vs Claude Joseph: one killing, different judicial roles

A survivor and a former prime minister appear at different judicial stages. Election registration neither erases those records nor supplies a verdict on them.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-15

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

Martine Moïse and Claude Joseph appear in the presidential race, but their names in the Jovenel Moïse assassination record do not give them identical roles. Voters need responsibilities, allegations and decisions placed in sequence.

The U.S. Justice Department’s May 8, 2026 verdict statement describes Martine as the first lady seriously wounded during the July 7, 2021 attack. The State Department’s statement on the assassination date identifies Joseph as acting prime minister. A survivor’s injuries and a government leader’s responsibilities are different starting points. AP reported her testimony at four men’s Miami trial in March 2026. [1] [2] [3]

Vant Bèf Info reports that the earlier 2024 order referred both for criminal trial over alleged complicity. The same source reports that the Port-au-Prince appeals court annulled that order on October 13, 2025 and required renewed investigation. BINUH’s July–September 2026 report confirms further hearings in the supplementary inquiry during July and August. [4] [5]

That prevents two shortcuts: repeating the old order without its annulment, or treating annulment as acquittal after trial. In the United States, the May 8 verdict concerns Arcangel Pretel Ortiz, Antonio Intriago, Walter Veintemilla and James Solages. It is not a conviction of Martine Moïse or Claude Joseph. A multi-defendant case does not transfer guilt from one person to another. [1]

This does not summarize every transcript and decision. Registration does not settle the inquiry either. Political scrutiny should follow each candidate’s public acts and programme; criminal scrutiny requires evidence and decisions about that specific person. Without that distinction, voters can mistake a mentioned name for a verdict.
