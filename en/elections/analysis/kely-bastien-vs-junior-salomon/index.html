Kely Bastien vs Junior Salomon: a Senate title does not explain all the power

Their records raise different questions: how a leader accounts for his decisions, and how he obtains authority to lead.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-15

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

Senate experience does not, by itself, explain how someone exercised power. Kely Bastien and Jean-Marie Junior Salomon provide two examples requiring different records: the administration of an institution and the rules granting authority to lead it.

A briefing in a 2011 trip report hosted by the U.S. Senate Foreign Relations Committee identifies Bastien as a northern senator and records his election as Senate president in 2008. It presents transparency and better relations between branches as his objectives. Those are documented aims, not an audit confirming delivery. [1]

For Salomon, Rezo Nòdwès’s January 13, 2020 report describes his announcement that he was provisionally assuming the Senate presidency during the dispute over terms. He cited the Senate’s permanent character under Constitution Article 95-1. Secretary-general Rony Gilot recalled rules on electing the bureau and an interim role for the oldest member. [2]

An institution’s continuity and an individual’s authority to lead it are different questions. For Bastien, scrutiny follows budgets, expenditure and publication of decisions during his responsibilities. For Salomon, it requires procedures, votes and minutes explaining the authority claimed. These two reports cannot adjudicate every legal dispute over the Senate.

A candidate advocating respect for institutions must also explain which procedures he accepts when they limit his own power. That connects the records: rules must operate both when they help a leader and when they prevent him doing what he wants.
