Martelly and the $1.50 fee: what were families paying for?

Under Martelly, a transfer fee became part of a promise of free schooling. A lawsuit later challenged how the arrangement worked.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-15

Every time a family pays the $1.50 transfer fee, less money remains for other needs. Ten payments of this fee cost $15, before the company’s other service charges.

The arrangement began under President Michel Martelly. Haiti’s central bank’s annual report confirms that Circular 98 established a US$1.50 fee on incoming and outgoing transfers, effective June 1, 2011. [1]

The promise was to help children attend school free of charge. A 2023 U.S. court decision describes videos in which Martelly and then-Digicel chairman Denis O’Brien explained the education-funding plan. A small transfer fee had become part of a large political promise. [2]

How much money did that produce? Haiti’s audit court supplies a first answer in its report for 2017–2018. It reports about US$120 million collected through the central bank from transfer fees in 2011–2018 for the National Education Fund, or FNE. Nearly $118 million went to the Treasury, which manages government money. The table records about $2.37 million remaining at the period’s end. [3]

There is therefore an official trail into government accounts. The report also describes school construction and rehabilitation. But the court reserves the right to check classroom numbers and construction quality through site visits. [3]

That still leaves each family’s question: what did my fees achieve? A Treasury transfer does not automatically become a school place. Assessing Martelly’s promise requires connecting government receipts to spending, schools and the children who benefited. Accountability has to reach that last step.
