School-fee money: the accounting problem continued

A report covering 2023–2024 says FNE revenue was counted in the government budget balance while its spending was left out. Why does that matter?

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-15

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

A family needs to count both income and spending to know what remains. Count the income but leave out the related spending, and the balance looks larger than it is.

Haiti’s audit court identifies this kind of problem in government accounts for fiscal year 2023–2024. Its report says the Finance Ministry counted resources intended for the National Education Fund in the budget balance but left out FNE expenditure. The court says this distorted the reported surplus. [1]

This is the education fund appearing in the transfer-fee trail since 2011. The court’s 2017–2018 report had already described money collected through the central bank and CONATEL and transferred to the Treasury. Reading the reports together raises a further question: do government accounts let the public follow education money through to its final use? [2] [1]

There are public records to examine. FNE’s website lists financial assistance for 2025–2026, with beneficiaries, purposes and amounts. It provides a starting point for checking payments. An institution’s own list, however, cannot replace an independent audit or verification of what schools received. [3]

This accounting problem does not prove that Martelly or another former president took the money. The report for 2023–2024 covers a period after his presidency. It shows why an education promise needs more than collection figures: it needs accounts showing what government did with the money.
