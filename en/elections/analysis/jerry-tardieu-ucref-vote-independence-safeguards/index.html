Tardieu’s UCREF vote: who should control the money investigators?

His account celebrated a bill. A rights organisation warned about independence. The missing legislative chain matters to both claims.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-18

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

On May 5, 2017, Tardieu’s account celebrated the vote on the UCREF bill and thanked special-commission colleagues. UCREF is the financial-intelligence unit: it examines suspicious financial information, a function relevant to tracing concealed money. [1] [2]

RNDDH’s May report dates lower-house and Senate votes to May 4 and May 8. It criticises appointment powers and institutional safeguards. The organisation’s analysis does not name Tardieu and is not a judicial finding against him. [2]

The date sequence supports a narrower reading of the May 5 celebration: the lower house had acted while the Senate stage was still ahead. The harder issue is the institution’s design. If investigators depend on political appointers, what protects inquiries involving those appointers or their allies?

RNDDH compares the 2001 and 2017 texts. The wider framework also includes a 2013 law and a 2016 amendment. Counting fewer articles cannot alone establish that a protection disappeared rather than moved elsewhere. Original statutes, amendments and the commission’s explanations are needed to test that question. [2] [3]

A candidate claiming reform experience can answer concretely: which powers changed, which guarantees remained, how did he vote and what was his reasoning? Modernising an agency and making it politically dependent are competing interpretations. The public record should let citizens distinguish them without treating criticism as a conviction.
