Tardieu’s 2018 reform: who gets power when legislators stay away?

Two passages in the commission report offer different answers to the number needed for Parliament to meet.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-18

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

The August 2018 constitutional proposals name Jerry Tardieu as commission chair. One passage would reduce the attendance requirement to one-third after repeated calls. Another allows deliberation after two failed sittings regardless of attendance. A later section again specifies one-third. Both formulations preserve a reference to special-majority cases. [1]

Imagine a fictional chamber with 90 members. One-third means 30 must attend. A rule allowing any attendance supplies no equivalent numerical floor. That illustration describes the difference between the proposals, not the size of Haiti’s chamber or an actual vote.

The political bargain is clear. Requiring many members can let absences block work. Lowering the threshold lets business continue, but can give a smaller group authority over people whose representatives are missing. Solving obstruction requires deciding how much representation can be sacrificed and for which decisions.

The retrieved copy is a proposal, not enacted law or a verified final article-by-article version. Its internal mismatch may reflect unmerged alternatives or drafting errors. It does not establish that Tardieu deliberately sought minority rule.

Which version did he ultimately support? Which votes would still require broader attendance? What stops a majority arranging repeated failures to reach the easier rule? Those answers matter more than calling a reform efficient. The attendance rule decides who can act in everybody’s name.
