Olivier Barrau: what AIC owns and what it owes

The 2022 balance sheet separates what the company holds, owes and retains for shareholders. It is not the candidate’s personal wealth.

Ayiti Sitwayen · Updated: 2026-11-16

An examination of documented information, with interpretations clearly identified.

A company can hold substantial assets while carrying substantial obligations. Calling all those assets its leader’s wealth erases part of the accounts. That matters when Olivier Barrau’s AIC experience enters the presidential debate.

AIC’s 2022 balance sheet is in thousands of gourdes. It gives total assets of 4,205,550 and liabilities of 2,755,231: approximately 4.206 billion gourdes in assets and 2.755 billion in liabilities. We neither read thousands as single gourdes nor convert them into dollars without an exchange-rate basis. [1]

The report gives approximately 1.450 billion gourdes in all shareholders’ equity. That does not assign the entire amount to Barrau. It identifies him as board president, but is not a complete declaration of his ownership percentage or current wealth. [1] [2]

The report’s audit letter says the accounts fairly present the company’s position in material respects. That has a specific meaning for company accounts at that date. It is not an audit of the campaign, all personal interests or a government he has not led. [1]

Voters still need declared ownership, who would manage it during a term and which state decisions could affect the company. Reading accounts properly makes that scrutiny possible without turning a large balance sheet into invented wealth or an accusation.
